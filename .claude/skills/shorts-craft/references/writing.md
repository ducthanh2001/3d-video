# Writing: clever, short, on target (scripts, captions, punch lines, titles)

Adapted from cinecode `references/writing.md` (MIT, Sarp Yildiz) for wordless 30–45 s motivational Shorts (US audience).
A Short has room for one idea and very few words.

## 1. Diverge, then converge (private)

1. Write 10 angles for the idea (vary point of view, scale, time, the physical task the pair performs).
2. **Discard the first 3:** they are what anyone would make. Also drop any idea on the competitors' used-ideas list
   (docs/claude-memory/competitor-format.md, competitor-top4-breakdown.md).
3. Keep the 3 most **surprising-yet-true** (a first-time viewer would not predict it; afterwards they say "of course").
4. Offer those 3 to the user with a one-line reason each, recommended first.

## 2. One turn, ending first

- Write **Before: … / After: …** for the film and for each vignette (the easy option looks better → the cost shows).
- Write the **last image and last line first**; everything it needs (a count, a rule, an object) is set up in the first third.
- The hook is a **question the film owes**, answered by the ending (TRUST: "How many reached the top?" → "1 out of 100").

## 3. Show, don't tell

- **The caption test:** a line that only names what the shot shows is cut or flipped into what the picture cannot say
  (a count, a rule, the inner voice, a contrast).
- **Never explain the moral** in a sentence the image already proves; fix the image instead.
- **Muted test:** with sound off, the viewer can say what happened in one sentence.

## 4. Budget and lines

- On-screen card ≤ 6 words; a line ≤ 9 words; one idea per line; the punch is the shorter, second line.
- On screen at least 0.6 s + 0.25 s per word; the key word lands within 0.5 s of the image that answers it.
- Concrete nouns and verbs from this scene's world (rope, log, swing, 8,400 m) beat abstract words (effort, success).
- At most one adjective per noun, and only one the picture does not show.
- Leave the first second to the picture; a line starts 0.3–0.6 s after a cut.
- **Two passes:** read it aloud at speaking pace; then cut 30% of the words and keep the cut unless meaning breaks.

## 5. Banned (clichés and words that read as generated)

in a world where · journey (as a metaphor) · little did they know · what if…? · more than just a · every moment counts ·
against all odds · it's not about X, it's about Y · nothing would ever be the same · the rest is history ·
a story of hope / love / courage · where X meets Y · magic happens · follow your dreams / your heart ·
embark on / unlock / dive into · a testament to / a tapestry of · one thing is certain · timeless ·
an unforgettable experience · and then everything changed.

Also: **no em/en dash in the film's words** (use a full stop, a comma or a line break); no "not X, but Y" when nobody
claimed X; no deep-sounding sayings ("at its core", "here's the thing", "let's"); no inflated words (vibrant, pivotal,
seamless, breathtaking, whispers); no three-abstract-noun lists ("hope, courage and love"); no rhetorical question to the
viewer except the one hook question the film answers; at most two exclamation marks per film.

## 6. First-time viewer check (cold)

- Every "I / you / they / it / this" has exactly one referent on screen.
- Every effect has a cause the viewer saw (why did the counter drop? the viewer saw them sit down).
- What the ending needs is already in the film; nothing is left hanging.

## 7. Titles, descriptions, thumbnails

- Title: the film's promise in plain words (≤ 60 characters reads in full); one concrete number or contrast beats a slogan.
- Thumbnail text ≠ title text: the thumbnail carries the number or the labelled contrast (QUIT / DIDN'T, 1 OUT OF 100).
- Description: what happens in the order it happens, one comment prompt, 6–9 hashtags; no banned words.
