The average American checks their phone two hundred and five times a day. That's once every five minutes you're awake.
Meet June. She's about to check it again.
She checks it at the bus stop, in the elevator, and in the middle of brushing her teeth.
Then comes a spectacular sunset. The whole sky turns orange. June takes one photo, then spends the next ten minutes scrolling.
By the time she looks up, it's dark, and the sky has already finished.
Then her phone dies. The good news: nothing buzzes. The bad news: nothing buzzes.
So she buys a film camera. Twenty-four photos, no screen, no delete button. She can't check the shot, and she can't retake it. She just has to trust it.
Now every picture costs something, so she looks before she shoots. A flour handprint on a bakery door. A dog in a tiny sweater. The bridge at five o'clock.
It's the difference between an all-you-can-eat buffet and one small plate. When everything is unlimited, you stop tasting it.
Weeks later, the roll comes back from the lab. Twenty-four photos, and she remembers taking every one.
She still has her phone. But now, when the sky turns orange, it stays in her pocket.
